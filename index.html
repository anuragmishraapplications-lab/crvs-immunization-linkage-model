What could linking birth registries to immunization programmes deliver?

Where registration is high but immunization lags, the state already knows these children exist. The registry can be used as a find-list for the ones missing vaccines. This model sizes that opportunity conservatively and prices it against published evidence.

The scenario arithmetic

For each country the source sheet gives two marginal rates: birth registration and DPT3 immunization. It does not give the join between them. Without that cross-tabulation a point estimate is not available, so the model brackets the answer.

Ceiling (optimistic) = Birth registration − Immunization CLASS C
Assumes every unvaccinated child is registered, so the registry can find all of them and coverage climbs to the registration rate.
Floor (conservative) = (Birth registration − Immunization) − (100 − Birth registration) CLASS C
Assumes every unregistered child is also unvaccinated, so that whole slice is invisible to the registry and is stripped out of the headroom.

Worked through: Gabon registers 89.6% of births and immunizes 61%. The gap is 29 points, the unregistered share 10, so even in the worst case linkage is worth about 19 points. Honduras registers 97% and immunizes 75% — a 22-point gap against only 3 points unregistered, so the floor is 19 again, this time capturing nearly the whole gap. That convergence is the point: the higher registration goes, the less the conservative assumption costs you.

Turning coverage points into money

Two published parameters multiplied. A benefit–cost ratio for childhood immunization in low- and middle-income countries, and a cost per fully immunized child that converts the ratio into an absolute figure.

Children reached = Floor (pp) ÷ 100 × annual birth cohort
Gross benefit = Children reached × benefit per child
Net benefit = Gross benefit − (Children reached × delivery cost)

Three assumptions worth stating out loud

Average cost is not marginal cost CLASS C

Published ROI ratios are programme averages that include easy-to-reach children. This intervention targets the children the system has been missing, who cost substantially more to find. Benefit per child is broadly unchanged — disease burden averted is a property of the child, not of how hard they were to reach — but cost rises, which is why the model nets out a marginal delivery cost rather than applying the headline ratio unadjusted. On the defaults this pulls the effective return to roughly 7× rather than the published 20×.

DPT3 stands in for the full schedule CLASS C

Benefit figures assume a child the system reaches completes the whole infant schedule, not only the DPT doses. DPT3 is the standard proxy for a child the system has genuinely reached, which makes this defensible, but a strict floor counting only diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis burden would be materially smaller.

The registry only helps if identification is the constraint CLASS C

Every number here assumes a child is unvaccinated because the programme could not find them. Where the real barrier is conflict, supply failure or economic collapse, the registry can locate the child and change nothing. That is why several high-gap countries are set aside by default rather than counted, and why that judgement is a toggle rather than a hidden filter.

Data provenance still open

Birth registration and DPT3 rates are used as supplied in the source sheet. Reference years and definitions for the two series still need confirming with Philip — the plot inherits whatever vintage mismatch exists between them, and any figure leaving this model should carry the confirmed years.

The scenario arithmetic

For each country the pipeline gives two marginal rates: the share of deaths registered, and the share of people above pensionable age receiving a pension. It does not give the join between them. Without that cross-tabulation a point estimate is not available, so the model brackets the answer.

Overlap ceiling (optimistic) = MIN(registration, pension coverage) CLASS C
Assumes registration and pension receipt coincide as far as arithmetic permits, so every pensioner's death is a registered death.
Overlap floor (conservative) = MAX(0, registration + pension coverage − 100) CLASS C
Assumes the two groups avoid each other as far as arithmetic permits. Whatever is left over must overlap, because the two shares are together too large to fit inside 100.

Worked through: a country registering 90% of deaths and paying a pension to 80% of older people must have at least 70% of its deaths falling on pension recipients, because the two groups are too large to avoid one another. At most 80% can, which happens only if every pensioner's death is registered. The truth sits in a ten-point band and the floor holds regardless.

The bracket is unusually tight here. Across the assessable countries the ceiling exceeds the floor by a median of about 1.00×, and by more than 2× for only four of them, because the bound collapses whenever either rate approaches 100. That is worth saying plainly: the analytically hard part of this model turns out not to be where the uncertainty lives.

Turning deaths into money

Unlike a vaccination, a stopped payment needs no benefit–cost literature to value it. The saving is the payment itself. What the model must supply instead is how many payments continue, and for how long.

Linkable deaths = deaths aged 65+ × overlap ÷ 100
Timely and matchable = linkable deaths × timely registration ÷ 100 × match rate ÷ 100
Annual flow exposure = timely and matchable × monthly pension × months avoided × legally stoppable ÷ 100
First-year sweep = beneficiaries × already-deceased share ÷ 100 × monthly pension × 12 × legally stoppable ÷ 100

The flow recurs every year. The sweep does not: it clears a backlog that has accumulated once and cannot accumulate again while the linkage runs. They are reported separately for that reason, and the sweep should never be annualised.

Four assumptions worth stating out loud

Pension coverage is measured among the living CLASS C

The share of older people receiving a pension is measured across the living population above pensionable age. This model applies it to people who die. No publisher cross-tabulates pension receipt against deaths, so the substitution is forced by the absence of data rather than chosen — but if pension recipients die at a materially different rate from non-recipients, and in most countries they will, the figure is biased in a direction this model cannot determine. Replace it with a historical death-to-beneficiary match wherever programme data allow.

Deaths at 65 and over are not pension-age deaths CLASS C

Statutory pension ages differ, sometimes by a decade and often by sex, and not everyone dying at 65 or over was ever eligible. The underlying dataset holds deaths by five-year age band, so a country-specific threshold can be substituted once reliable retirement-age data are in hand. Until then this is an approximation applied uniformly, which flatters countries with a low pension age and penalises those with a high one.

Countries are not measured on a like-for-like basis CLASS C

Most rows use registered deaths divided by expected deaths; a few use cause-of-death completeness. The second measures whether a doctor certified a cause, not whether a death was legally registered, and it can understate registration badly. India appears here at roughly 10% on that basis while its civil registration of deaths is far higher. Every row states which measure it used, and the first tab can set the completeness-only countries aside entirely.

The register only helps if the payroll does not already know CLASS D

Every number here assumes payment continued because the pension system had not learned of the death. Where families already report deaths promptly to claim survivor benefits, or where proof-of-life verification runs frequently, the linkage adds little and the months-avoided assumption should fall towards zero. That is why the delay reduction is a slider rather than a fixed parameter, and why the model reports the reduction rather than the full delay.

Data provenance still open

The largest open item is the death-registration measure. A published national coverage series per country would replace the derived ratio for 140 countries and lift that input from Class C to Class B; the UN Statistics Division Demographic Yearbook carries completeness-of-death-registration tables that have not yet been assembled into a comparable panel. Second is the average pension: 108 assessable countries have no defensible amount, mostly because ASPIRE publishes no beneficiary count for their social pension programmes. Third, and most valuable, is a single country willing to run its own death register against its own payment file, which would convert the entire chain from a bracket into an observation.
